What this app covers
  • Today's data: about 2,700 US-listed common stocks worth at least $300M, on NYSE, Nasdaq and NYSE American, including the S&P 500 and ADRs of foreign companies that report in US dollars. S&P 500 members use official GICS sectors; the rest use Yahoo Finance's sector, mapped to the matching GICS name. Used for sector benchmarks, sector ranges, the Companies table and holdings lookups.
  • History back to 2010 (sector charts over time, valuation vs. history, sector ideas, the allocation and past dates): the 503 S&P 500 members only, because history is rebuilt from SEC filings for those companies.
  • Not covered: ETFs and funds (they can be entered as holdings and appear as "ETFs & other"), companies under $300M, shell companies (SPACs), warrants, units and preferred shares, companies reporting in another currency (e.g. TSMC, ASML, Spotify), and OTC stocks. About 5% of eligible companies may be missing when their Yahoo Finance lookup fails during a data build.

Positive / Negative / Neutral is scored from each headline's wording with a finance word list (e.g. "beats", "upgrade", "record high" against "misses", "probe", "price target cut"), counting "not" and "despite"; hover a tag to see the words it used. Sector allocation

Weights: all US $300M+ · tilt: S&P 500 backtest

Add your holdings with their market values above to see your sector mix next to the backtest-based allocation.
Show as table
Your holdings are grouped by each stock's sector; ETFs, funds and stocks this app doesn't cover go in "ETFs & other", since a fund's sector mix isn't known here.
Backtest-based allocation: each sector starts at its share of the total market value of every US-listed stock worth $300M or more that the app covers (the "market weight"). Each sector's reading comes from its S&P 500 members, since history exists only for them, and applies to the whole sector. Sectors reading Above history in the sector ideas section get 1.5 times that weight, sectors reading Below history get 0.5 times, and the rest are unchanged; the weights are then rescaled to add up to 100%.
Why this direction: the multipliers come from a backtest of S&P 500 sectors from July 2011 to 2026 (sector weights and SPDR sector ETFs, run October 20, 2026). Over that period, sectors trading above their own history did slightly better than market weights and sectors below it slightly worse, so the opposite "favor cheap sectors" rule (1.5x below, 0.5x above) trailed.
Track record: against simply holding the market weights, this tilt returned +0.04% over the following 13 weeks, +0.18% over 26 and +0.30% over 52, and came out ahead on 55%, 58% and 58% of test dates. That edge is small, about the size of its margin of error (±0.17% over 26 weeks). It held in 2011–2015, 2016–2019 and 2023–2026 but reversed in 2020–2022, when cheaper sectors rebounded. Finer-tuned multipliers did not help: the best combination on 2011–2018 lost money on 2019–2026, while 0.5x / 1.5x gained, so the app keeps round numbers. The test uses today's S&P 500 members throughout (survivorship bias) and starts in 2011, when the first readings are available.
This is an arithmetic illustration of the screen's rule, not investment advice. It knows nothing about your goals, time horizon, taxes or other investments, and the holdings you enter are not stored or sent anywhere.

How they are chosen: the cheapest sector is the one furthest below its own 5-year median across LTM P/E, EV/EBITDA and EV/Revenue; the most expensive is the one furthest above. Each must be more than 5% away from its median on at least two of the three multiples. Trend is context only and does not change the ranking. Headlines come from Yahoo Finance for the sector's SPDR ETF, as of the last data pull, and are not shown for past dates.
What happened next, historically: in a backtest from July 2011 to 2026 (run October 20, 2026, using SPDR sector ETFs), cheap did not mean "about to rise". The cheapest sector trailed the S&P 500 on average over the following 13, 26 and 52 weeks (by 1.4%, 3.1% and 4.8%); the most expensive also trailed, but by less (0.9%, 0.8% and 2.6%). That is why the sector allocation leans toward sectors above their history, not below. Any single sector is riskier than the whole index.
This is the output of a mechanical screen for study purposes, not investment advice, and it takes no account of anyone's circumstances.
